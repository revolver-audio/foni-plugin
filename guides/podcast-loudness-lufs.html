How to hit −16 LUFS for Apple Podcasts (and −14 for Spotify / YouTube)

Short answer: aim for −16 LUFS integrated with true peak at or below −1 dBTP. That suits Apple Podcasts and plays fine everywhere else. Here is what the numbers mean and three free ways to get there.

Apple recommends keeping the overall loudness around −16 LKFS (the same unit as LUFS) with ±1 dB tolerance, and true peak no higher than −1 dBFS (Apple Podcasts audio requirements). Spotify normalizes playback to −14 LUFS following ITU-R BS.1770 (Spotify loudness normalization). YouTube does not publish an official podcast target; −14 LUFS is the value most creators use.

Which one should I pick? If you publish everywhere, use −16 LUFS. Spotify turns quieter files up only as far as their peaks allow, so a −16 episode may play a little quieter there than a −14 one; the difference is small. If you publish only on YouTube or Spotify, −14 is fine.

LUFS and true peak in one minute

  • LUFS measures how loud audio sounds to people, averaged over the whole episode. It is not the same as the peak meter in your editor.
  • Integrated means "over the whole file". A single loud laugh barely changes it; a quiet second half does.
  • True peak (dBTP) is the highest level the audio reaches after it is turned back into sound, including peaks between samples. Keeping it at −1 dBTP leaves room for MP3 and AAC encoding.

Three free ways to get there

1. Audacity: Loudness Normalization

  1. Edit your episode first (cuts, music, levels between speakers).
  2. Select all, then use Audacity's built-in Loudness Normalization effect and set it to −16 LUFS.
  3. Add a limiter at the end with the ceiling at −1 dB, then export.

This sets the average, but it does not fix a guest who is much quieter than the host, or a sentence you trail off at the end of. Normalization moves the whole file up or down by one amount.

2. Any DAW: a loudness meter plus a limiter

Put a free loudness meter on the master, play the whole episode, and raise or lower the gain until the integrated reading lands on −16. A true-peak limiter at −1 dBTP catches the peaks. Accurate, but slow: you have to play the full episode to read the integrated value.

Common mistakes

  • Normalizing peaks instead of loudness. "Normalize to −1 dB" in an editor sets the loudest peak, not the loudness. Two episodes normalized this way can be 6 dB apart.
  • Measuring a short clip. Integrated loudness needs the whole episode. Measure the final export.
  • Hitting the target with a quiet guest. The average can be −16 while the guest sits 8 dB under the host. Level the voices first, then set loudness.
  • Limiting to 0 dB. Without −1 dBTP of headroom, MP3 encoding can clip.
